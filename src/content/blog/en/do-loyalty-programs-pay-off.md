**In short:** Loyalty programs pay off primarily because retention is dramatically cheaper than acquisition, and the data from Bain, Antavo and the 2026 DACH Loyalty Report all point the same way: keeping customers is where the profit is.

"Is a loyalty program actually worth it, or is it just a free coffee I give away?" It is a fair question, and it deserves a numbers answer rather than a cheerful one. Fortunately, retention is one of the most studied topics in business, and the research is unusually consistent. Here is what it says.

## Keeping a customer is far cheaper than finding one

Start with the cost side, because this is where most of the value hides. Bain & Company's long-running work on customer retention found that **acquiring a new customer costs five to twenty-five times more than retaining an existing one.** The exact multiple depends on the industry, but even at the low end, the gap is enormous.

Think about what acquisition actually costs you: advertising, introductory discounts, the time spent getting a stranger's attention, and the fact that many of them never come back after the first visit. Retention costs a fraction of that, because the person already knows you, already likes you, and only needs a reason to return. **A loyalty program is exactly that reason, delivered cheaply and repeatedly.**

## Small increases in retention move profit a lot

The most cited figure in this field is also from Bain: **increasing customer retention by just five percent can raise profit by anywhere from 25 to 95 percent.** That is not a typo, and it is not a small lever. A modest improvement in how many customers come back compounds into a large improvement in the bottom line, because retained customers cost less to serve and tend to spend more over time.

That "spend more over time" part is not a hunch either. Bain's analysis found that repeat customers increase their spending per visit as the relationship deepens, in some categories buying on the order of **67 percent more per visit** than they did as newcomers. Loyalty is not just about frequency. It is about each visit being worth more.

## Customers themselves say programs change their behavior

The business data is backed up by what consumers report about their own choices. The 2026 DACH Loyalty Report, published by hello again together with the Handelsverband, surveyed how a loyalty program changes shopping behavior:

- **29.3 percent** of consumers say they shop more often at a business that offers a loyalty program.
- **27.9 percent** say they spend more at a business where a program exists.

In other words, for roughly one in four customers, the mere presence of a loyalty program shifts both how often they come and how much they spend. That is a meaningful slice of your customer base changing behavior in your favor, for the cost of a well-designed card and the occasional free item.
> Source: hello again × Handelsverband, DACH Loyalty Report 2026.

## The return on investment, measured

If you want a single headline number, Antavo's 2025 loyalty research provides one: in the DACH region, loyalty programs delivered an **average return of 6.2x.** Every euro invested in the program returned about six.

Treat that as a direction, not a promise, because your result depends on execution: a program nobody knows about returns nothing. But the figure lines up with everything above. When retention is five to twenty-five times cheaper than acquisition, a five percent retention gain can nearly double profit, and a quarter of customers say a program makes them spend more, a six-fold return is not surprising. It is what you would expect when you stop paying full price for every customer and start reusing the ones you already earned.
> Source: Antavo, Global Customer Loyalty Report 2025.

## What this means for a stamp card specifically

You do not need an enterprise points system to capture this. A simple digital stamp card is retention in its most direct form:

- It gives existing customers a concrete reason to return, which is the whole retention lever.
- It costs almost nothing per customer, so the acquisition-versus-retention math works strongly in your favor.
- It keeps you visible between visits when it lives in Apple Wallet or Google Wallet, so the relationship does not go cold.

The mistake is treating the free tenth coffee as a cost. It is not a cost. It is the cheapest customer you will acquire all year, because it is a customer you already had, coming back for a reason you gave them.

## Häufig gestellte Fragen

### Is a loyalty program actually worth the effort for a small business?

The numbers say yes, mostly because keeping a customer is far cheaper than finding a new one. Bain & Company found that acquiring a new customer costs five to twenty-five times more than retaining an existing one, and that raising retention by just five percent can lift profit by 25 to 95 percent. A loyalty program is one of the cheapest ways to move that retention number.

### How much more do loyal customers actually spend?

According to Bain & Company, repeat customers spend more per visit over time, in some categories on the order of 67 percent more than new ones. The 2026 DACH Loyalty Report adds that 27.9 percent of consumers say they spend more where a loyalty program exists, and 29.3 percent say they shop there more often.

### What return on investment can a loyalty program deliver?

Antavo's 2025 research reported an average return of 6.2x on loyalty programs in the DACH region, meaning every euro invested returned about six. Your result depends heavily on execution, but the direction is clear: well-run retention beats spending the same money on acquisition.

### Why is retention cheaper than acquisition?

You have already paid to acquire an existing customer once. Bringing them back costs little more than a good reason to return, which is exactly what a loyalty program provides. Winning a new customer means paying again for advertising, discounts and attention, which Bain estimates at five to twenty-five times the cost of retaining one you already have.

## The takeaway

The question is not really "does loyalty pay off." The data has answered that. The real question is whether you would rather pay five to twenty-five times over to keep replacing customers, or a fraction of that to keep the ones you have. A stamp card is simply the simplest way to choose the cheaper side of that equation.
